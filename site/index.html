Special education compliance · Medicaid claiming

Sixty days from
a parent’s signature.

Federal law gives a school district sixty days to complete a special education evaluation. Miss it and the district owes compensatory services and faces a due process complaint. The person holding that clock is usually one coordinator with a spreadsheet and no compliance team.

The clock runs from the day the agency receives consent, not the day the parent signed it. Breaks longer than five days pause it in some states; others count school days instead, or thirty business days. Washington moved to thirty-five school days in June 2026. Every one of those is a different arithmetic on the same form.

The pairing

Compliance is a cost centre. Cost centres get cut.

Districts can bill Medicaid for the same services these deadlines govern, and most underclaim badly. A single New York City audit found $431.6 million unclaimed. The reimbursement requirements are, almost word for word, what a compliance system already holds: the service is in the IEP, it was delivered as written, and it was documented.

01  /  Keeps the clock

Recomputes every deadline, hourly, unattended

Under that student’s own jurisdiction rule and that district’s calendar. Escalates at fourteen, seven and two days out, firing the tightest applicable rung once rather than three notices at a case found late. Drafts the statutory notice and queues it for a named human.

02  /  Recovers the money

Asks whether a session would survive an audit

Eligibility, NPI, licence valid on the service date, provider type, IEP window, units against documented minutes — and whether the session note actually describes the authorised service. Over-billing blocks. Under-billing is surfaced as revenue left behind.

How it runs

A document arrives. Nobody presses anything.

01

A coordinator drops the form as it arrived

Phone-photo OCR noise, forwarded email, all of it. The page that accepts it has no model access at all — it screens the document and parks it. Anything carrying an embedded instruction is refused before an extractor ever sees it, because the screen sits in front of the model rather than after.

02

The fleet reads it on the next tick

It extracts the dates and returns nothing rather than guessing when a date is illegible — a statutory clock started from an invented date is worse than one a human is asked to confirm. Every case page shows which date started the clock and why.

03

It escalates down a privilege chain

Casework holds full clinical access to draft the legal notice. A separate model strips every clinical finding. Only then does the family-facing agent receive a redacted projection and write the letter home, which is spoken aloud in the family’s language.

04

A person decides whether it sends

The fleet drafts and queues. It never decides to contact a family. Nothing reaches a parent without an approver on the record, by name.

What holds it

They need an agent more than anyone. They will never be given one.

The records are minors’ clinical files and the district has no security staff. So the interesting half was never the agents — it was the guardrails that make pointing them at real records defensible.

The family-facing agent does not receive clinical fields and decline to use them. It never receives them. A check can be forgotten at a new call site; a projection cannot leak a field it never returned. Field classification fails closed, so a field nobody thought about is withheld rather than exposed.

Honest limits

Stated plainly, because several read as finished from the architecture alone.

Multi-tenancy
None. Every collection is global, so a second district would read the first one’s cases. One district is fine; two is a breach.
Agent identity
Registry-declared, not attested. What the gateway enforces is a scope table, not zero-trust.
Medicaid billing consent
Not modelled. IDEA §300.154 requires a consent to bill that is separate from consent to evaluate; the readiness gate does not yet check it.
State rules
Three of the four are invented stand-ins that exercise the counting engine. Only the federal sixty-day baseline is accurate.
Delivery
Implemented behind a human approval gate and exercised end to end, but the public demo runs the file driver and is read-only, so nothing leaves the machine.
Procurement
No SIS integration, no FERPA review, no signed data privacy agreement, and no SOC 2 — which 78% of district technology officers require.

What is real: it runs unattended on Google Cloud, the deadline arithmetic is correct and tested, the gate’s numbers are measured against real corpora, and the projection genuinely withholds clinical fields rather than asking an agent not to look at them.

That coordinator is never getting a compliance team.
